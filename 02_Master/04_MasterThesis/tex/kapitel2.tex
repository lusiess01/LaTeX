\chapter[Theoretical Background]{Theoretical Background}
This chapter provides the theoretical foundation for the development of the vibrotactile stimulation system.
It is divided into two main parts. The first part introduces the \textbf{medical background}, including neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s disease, their associated cognitive impairments, and current approaches to cognitive rehabilitation.
The second part focuses on the \textbf{technical background}, describing the principles of vibrotactile stimulation, actuation technologies, and existing systems.
This structure ensures that both the clinical motivation and the technical requirements are clearly outlined before presenting the system analysis and implementation in the following chapters.

\section{Medical Background}

\subsection{Neurodegenerative Diseases with Cognitive Impairments}
Neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease are among the leading causes of cognitive impairment in older adults. They are characterized by a progressive loss of neuronal structures and functions, which over time leads to marked deficits in memory, attention, executive abilities, and the capacity to manage everyday life \cite{Harvey.2019}.

\subsubsection{Alzheimer's Disease}
Alzheimer’s disease is the most common form of dementia, accounting for approximately \SI{60}{\%} to \SI{70}{\%} of all cases \cite{WorldHealthOrganization.2021}. According to the Alzheimer’s Association, more than 55 million people worldwide are currently affected \cite{Association.2025}. The disease is characterized by the pathological accumulation of proteins, including amyloid-$\beta$ plaques and $\tau$-protein-related neurofibrillary tangles, which lead to the progressive death of neurons \cite{Dubois.2016}.

The first noticeable symptoms usually involve a decline in episodic memory, the part of long-term memory responsible for personal experiences. As the disease advances, deficits in language, spatial orientation, and judgment become more pronounced. Alzheimer’s disease is long-lasting and progressive, eventually resulting in complete dependence on care in the later stages \cite{WorldHealthOrganization.2021}.

\subsubsection{Parkinson's Disease}
After discussing Alzheimer’s disease, the second major neurodegenerative disorder with cognitive impact is Parkinson’s disease. Parkinson’s disease is primarily known as a movement disorder, but cognitive symptoms are also frequent. Approximately \SI{30}{\%} to \SI{40}{\%} of patients develop Parkinson’s dementia during the course of the disease \cite{Janvin.2005}. Typical impairments include deficits in attention control, executive functions, and visuospatial perception, while memory is often less affected in the early stages \cite{Emre.2007}.

The underlying cause of the disease is a shortage of dopamine in the brain, resulting from the progressive death of neurons in the \textit{substantia nigra}. In addition, the accumulation of pathological protein deposits, known as Lewy bodies, can further contribute to the development of cognitive symptoms \cite{Kalia.2015}.

\subsubsection{Cognitive Deficits and Functional Impact}
In addition to these disease-specific aspects, it is important to consider the resulting cognitive deficits and their functional impact. Cognitive impairments in neurodegenerative diseases affect core functions such as memory, attention, language, visual processing, and executive abilities \cite{Harvey.2019}. These deficits have a direct impact on everyday activities, for example when taking medication independently, managing finances, maintaining social contacts, or navigating in space \cite{AmericanPsychiatricAssociation.2013}.

In addition to these practical limitations, emotional and psychosocial burdens are common. Patients often experience frustration, depressive moods, and social isolation, which increase the strain on both those affected and their families.

Therefore, the early detection of cognitive symptoms is of great importance. It provides the basis for targeted interventions and rehabilitation measures that aim to preserve independence and quality of life for as long as possible \cite{Petersen.2009}.

\subsection{Cognitive Rehabilitation: Concepts, Methods, and Target Groups}
Multidisciplinary approaches are an important part of cognitive rehabilitation \cite{Zucchella.2018}.
They combine cognitive training, motor therapy, music therapy, and sensory interventions.
The goal is to maintain the independence of patients for as long as possible and to reduce the psychosocial burden on both patients and caregivers.

In addition to traditional training methods, EEG-based biomarkers are increasingly used.
Indicators such as the Brain Symmetry Index (BSI) and the Laterality Coefficient (LC) allow an objective assessment of the brain’s functional state.
EEG analysis makes it possible to adapt rehabilitation strategies to the individual by detecting changes in brain activity, especially in connection with \textit{motor imagery}, which is an established rehabilitation method.
These approaches are mainly used with stroke patients, who often suffer from both motor and cognitive impairments.

Another important method within cognitive rehabilitation is sensory stimulation, with a growing focus on low-frequency vibration.
Campbell et al. \cite{Campbell.2022} report in their scoping review that vibrotactile interventions can improve attention, mood, and cognitive stability.
Depending on the application context, ranging from short-term interventions in nursing homes to long-term home-based programs, studies found positive outcomes in standardized cognitive tests as well as good patient acceptance.

In summary, cognitive rehabilitation is not limited to one single method but is based on a complementary, multimodal approach.
The integration of cognitive training, EEG-based biomarkers, and sensory methods such as vibrotactile stimulation forms a broad field of interventions that is likely to become more important in the future.
